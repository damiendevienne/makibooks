## Why this application? 

We started Maki Books in Heraklion after moving to Crete as a French family and realising we couldn’t bring all the books we wanted to keep reading. 📚 Finding books in French in Heraklion is almost impossible, so we created a simple way for local readers to lend and borrow books freely in any language. 🌍

## Why Maki Books?

The name **Maki Books** brings together several stories: a lemur from Madagascar, a highly social animal, an ancient Japanese form of storytelling, the history of illustrated natural-history books, and—quite simply—a name we find friendly and endearing.

- **Maki is a name for our lemur mascot**

  In French, *maki* is a vernacular name applied to several species of lemur from Madagascar. The word was borrowed from the Malagasy *maka* or *maky* and has been part of the French language for several centuries ([CNRTL](https://www.cnrtl.fr/definition/maki)).

  One of these animals is the **ring-tailed lemur**, *Lemur catta*, known in French as the *maki catta*. With its unmistakable black-and-white tail, bright eyes and expressive face, this is the species that inspired the Maki Books mascot ([Muséum national d’Histoire naturelle](https://www.mnhn.fr/en/the-ring-tailed-lemur)).

- **Maki is a social animal**

  Ring-tailed lemurs are highly social primates. They live in structured groups of around twenty individuals, usually led by a dominant adult female. They move together, communicate through a rich range of calls, postures and scents, and maintain important relationships within the group. Their conspicuous striped tails can even serve as visual landmarks for other group members when the troop is moving ([Muséum national d’Histoire naturelle](https://www.mnhn.fr/en/the-ring-tailed-lemur)).

  This social way of life reflects the idea behind Maki Books. The application is not merely a catalogue of available books: it is a community of people who meet, communicate and allow books to circulate between them.

- **In Japanese, Maki evokes an ancient form of storytelling**

  The name carries an entirely separate meaning in Japanese. In Japanese, *maki* (巻き) refers to something rolled or wound. The word is familiar today through *makizushi*—rolled sushi—but it also appears in the word **emaki**, or *emakimono*, used for Japanese illustrated narrative scrolls. Introduced to Japan through exchanges with China and associated with the spread of Buddhism, these long rolls of paper or silk developed into a distinctive form of Japanese visual storytelling. Text and images were revealed progressively as the reader unrolled the scroll with one hand and rolled it up again with the other ([Asia Society](https://asiasociety.org/education/emakimono)).

  Emaki narrated religious stories, legends, historical events, everyday life and works of literature. Some of the most celebrated examples are connected with major literary works such as *The Tale of Genji*. The movement of the scroll created its own reading rhythm: the story appeared gradually, scene after scene, rather than being visible all at once.

  **Note:** The Japanese and Malagasy words are not historically related—the similarity is a happy coincidence. But their two meanings meet beautifully in the name: one evokes our lemur mascot, while the other evokes the physical unfolding of stories.

- **Makis have a place in the history of natural-history books**

  The name also has a long-standing connection with books themselves, particularly with the European tradition of natural history.

  Makis appeared in the writings of some of the great French naturalists of the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries. Buffon discussed them in his monumental *Histoire naturelle*, helping to introduce these unfamiliar animals from Madagascar to European readers.

  In 1799 and 1800, the French naturalist and illustrator **Jean-Baptiste Audebert** published his remarkable *Histoire naturelle des singes et des makis*. The work combined zoological description with carefully produced illustrations and included a plate depicting the ring-tailed lemur under one of its former names, **“Le Mococo”** (see Figure below).

  Audebert’s engraving is more than a beautiful old image. It belongs to a period when scientific knowledge travelled through books, specimens, drawings and accounts from distant places. Long before wildlife photography, illustrated natural-history volumes allowed readers to encounter animals they would never see in person.

  This historical connection is especially meaningful for Maki Books: the same animal that serves as our modern digital mascot was already travelling between people more than two centuries ago—through the pages of a book.

- **And, finally, we simply like the name**

  We chose **Maki Books** because *Maki* is short, cheerful and easy to remember. Maki Books is about giving books another journey. Our maki is here to help them along the way.
